The challenge

Your AI product is ready for Europe. Is it ready for the EU AI Act?

Every company that sells an AI-powered product in the EU must now show that it meets the EU AI Act. Most product teams can't yet say whether they do.

The EU AI Act regulates AI by risk. What you must do depends on what your system does, who uses it, who it affects and where you sit in the AI value chain. For a team about to launch in Europe, compliance has become a product question as well as a legal one, and it usually comes up too late.

Three questions every team gets stuck on

01

Does this apply to us?

It is hard to tell whether a new or existing AI feature falls into a prohibited, high-risk, transparency or general-purpose AI (GPAI) category.

02

What exactly must we do?

Hundreds of pages of legal text have to be turned into concrete product, engineering, documentation and monitoring tasks.

03

Are we still compliant?

Obligations apply in phases and the dates keep shifting. Prohibitions and GPAI duties already apply, transparency rules have applied since August 2026, and standalone high-risk obligations move to December 2027.

The cost of getting it wrong

€35M or 7%of global annual turnover for prohibited AI practices

€15M or 3%for breaching other obligations

Up to these amounts, whichever is higher. Lower caps apply to SMEs.

AI regulation has become part of product management. Teams need a way to answer these questions before launch, not after a regulator asks.

Ancient wisdom, modern compliance

Two paths are in front of every AI team

The Katha Upanishad named this choice thousands of years ago.

Young Nachiketa stands before Yama, the lord of death. Yama offers him wealth, kingdoms and long life. Nachiketa turns them all down and asks for true knowledge. Yama praises him because he chose Shreyas, the lasting good, over Preyas, what is pleasant right now.

PreyasThe pleasant path

  • Ship now and deal with regulation later
  • “Our lawyers will look at it before launch”
  • Feels fast today
  • Gaps surface after deployment, as fines, recalls or lost deals
  • One-off, inconsistent reviews
vs

ShreyasThe right path

  • Build compliance into the product from day one
  • Every team understands its obligations as it builds
  • Stays fast, because nothing has to be redone
  • Gaps surface in design, when they are cheap to fix
  • A repeatable assessment across the whole AI portfolio

Aegis is the guide that makes Shreyas the easy choice.

  2. Classify

  4. Assess gaps
  5. Action plan

Classify

Works out whether the system is likely to be prohibited, high-risk, subject to transparency rules, GPAI-related or minimal risk.

Map obligations

Turns legal requirements into practical duties for risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight and security.

Check compliance

Recommend actions

Stay current

Tracks changing deadlines and Commission guidance so products can be reassessed over time.

Like Nachiketa, the wise team tells the two paths apart. Aegis helps you see the difference and take the right one.

Responsible by design

An AI that checks AI must hold itself to the same standard

We know where an LLM-based Copilot can fail. Here is how each risk is handled today, and what is still on the roadmap.

In place todayOn the roadmap

What could go wrongThe model states an obligation that doesn't exist or misreads an article.

In place today

  • Answers are grounded first in the document library (the Act's text), backed by a curated, article-level reference with links to EUR-Lex.
  • Every legal statement carries an inline citation, and a Sources box lists what was used.
  • Each classification comes with a confidence level (High, Medium or Low) and the fact that would change the answer.
  • If the sources don't cover something, Aegis says so instead of guessing.

On the roadmap

  • Add Commission guidance documents to the library, and sample answers for expert review.

What could go wrongA high-risk system is labeled minimal risk, or the reverse.

In place today

  • A fixed workflow checks in the Act's own order: scope, prohibited practices, high-risk (including the Article 6(3) exception and the profiling override), transparency, then GPAI. Tiers can stack.
  • Results are worded as “likely”. Missing facts become targeted questions.
  • High-stakes or ambiguous cases are sent to qualified counsel, not guessed.

On the roadmap

  • Rules-based decision logic for the classification itself, with the LLM in a supporting role, tested against labelled reference cases.

What could go wrongDeadlines and guidance change, as the 2026 amendments did.

In place today

  • The built-in reference carries a review date and has been updated for the Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), which moved the high-risk dates.
  • That date appears in the Terms and on every exported assessment.
  • Aegis compares each deadline with today's date and says whether it already applies.

On the roadmap

  • Automated monitoring for new acts and guidance, a version stamp on each assessment, and a flag for reassessment when the law changes.

What could go wrongTeams treat a Copilot result as a legal sign-off.

In place today

  • Clear positioning: Aegis is a first-line governance partner, not a lawyer or regulator.
  • Classifications use “likely” language, and every full assessment ends with a not-legal-advice line.
  • Serious situations (a possibly prohibited practice, an incident, an authority enquiry) are sent to counsel. The Terms say the same.

On the roadmap

  • A built-in sign-off step for high-risk conclusions.

What could go wrongProduct details shared with the Copilot leak or are used for training.

In place today

  • No accounts and no server-side conversation storage: chats stay in the user's own browser.
  • No analytics, and Aegis does not use conversations for training or advertising.
  • Users are told not to enter personal or confidential data and to describe systems in general terms. Messages are processed by AI providers under their own terms, and the Terms say so.

On the roadmap

  • An EU hosting region, automatic redaction of sensitive data, and encryption and role-based access if accounts are added.

What could go wrongThe same question gets different answers on different days.

In place today

  • One fixed system prompt, one curated reference, a fixed workflow and a fixed output template for assessments.
  • Automated tests cover the citation logic, the legal reference and the model routing.

On the roadmap

  • A regression suite of reference cases, run before every release, so the same system gets the same classification.

What could go wrongUsers try to manipulate the Copilot into unsafe or misleading output.

In place today

  • Every message passes a safety check before the model sees it.
  • Instructions to ignore override attempts, keep its instructions private, and refuse to help conceal non-compliance or mislead regulators.
  • Scope limited to EU AI Act topics, per-user rate limiting, and signed conversation summaries so earlier context can't be forged.

On the roadmap

  • Output-side guardrail checks and audit logging.

Why it mattersAegis is itself an AI system, so we follow the same rules we help others meet.

In place today

  • We tell users clearly that they are talking to an AI, in the welcome message and in the Terms.
  • We publish the Terms of Use and document how the Copilot works.

On the roadmap

  • Usage logging, and a formal human-review workflow for high-risk conclusions.
